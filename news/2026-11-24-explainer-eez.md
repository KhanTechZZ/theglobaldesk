# What Is an Exclusive Economic Zone, and Why Does It Matter?

An Exclusive Economic Zone, commonly known as an EEZ, is a maritime area in which a coastal country has specific rights over natural resources.

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, an EEZ can extend up to 200 nautical miles from a country's coastal baselines, subject to the rules of the Convention and the rights of other states.

These rights include exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing natural resources in the water, on the seabed and beneath it.

An EEZ is not the same as territorial waters.

In its territorial sea, a coastal state exercises sovereignty, subject to international law. In an EEZ, the state has sovereign rights over resources and certain specific jurisdictional responsibilities. Other countries retain certain freedoms, including navigation and overflight.

Why does this matter?

For countries with coastlines, an EEZ can be important for fisheries, offshore energy, marine research and resource management. It also makes maritime boundary agreements and international cooperation important.

An EEZ does not mean that a country owns the entire ocean within that zone. It means that international law grants it defined rights and responsibilities.
